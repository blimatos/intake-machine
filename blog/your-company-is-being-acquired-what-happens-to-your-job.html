Layoffs and mergersExplainer

Your company is being acquired. What happens to your job?

Usually nothing on day one. The decisions come over the next months, and the people who prepare early have the most options.

BBy Bruno, co-founderPublished Nov 3, 2026Updated Nov 3, 20262 min read

The short answer

When a deal is announced, your job, pay and manager usually stay the same until it closes, because the two companies must keep operating independently until then. After closing, the buyer maps both teams and reviews the roles that overlap first. Use the time: write down your results, learn the buyer and read your paperwork.

  • Nothing changes until the deal closes.
  • Roles that exist on both sides get reviewed first.
  • Write down your results now, with numbers.
  • Read your offer, retention and severance terms before you sign anything new.

What changes before the deal closes?

Very little, on purpose. Until the deal closes, the buyer and your company are still separate businesses, and US competition rules say they must keep operating independently. The Federal Trade Commission calls acting early "gun jumping", and it fines companies for it. Your contract, your pay and your manager stay as they are. What does change is the talk in the hallway, so treat rumors as rumors.

What happens after the deal closes?

The buyer starts the integration, and teams get mapped against each other. Much of the saving a buyer plans comes from removing work that both companies do twice, often in general and administrative functions like finance, HR and IT, and in duplicate management. The teams the buyer paid for (the product, the customers, the people who build it and sell it) are usually kept, and often grow.

Will I get notice if there are layoffs?

Often, yes. Under the federal WARN Act, employers with 100 or more employees must give 60 calendar days of written notice before a plant closing or a mass layoff of 50 or more people at one site. Some states ask for more. Smaller cuts can come with no notice at all, which is one more reason to get ready early.

What should I do in the first 30 days?

  1. Write down your results. Three to five, each with a number. You will need them for an internal interview or an outside one.
  2. Learn the buyer. How they run teams, who leads your area, and what they said about the deal.
  3. Read your paperwork. Your offer letter, any retention or severance terms, and your equity.
  4. Update your resume quietly. Being ready is not the same as leaving.

Questions people ask

Will I lose my job?

Not necessarily. Many roles carry over. Roles that exist on both sides, like finance, HR and IT, are the ones most often merged or cut.

Should I start applying elsewhere now?

Get ready now, decide later. Update your resume and your results, then watch what the buyer does with teams like yours.

What is a retention bonus?

Money paid to stay through the transition, usually until a set date. Read what you give up if you leave early.
