How to research your interviewer before an interview

Reading the job post twice is not the same as knowing who you are meeting. A little homework on the person across the table tells you which story to lead with, and which questions are coming.

Why research your interviewer?

An interview is a conversation with one person, and that person has a job to do. They own a product or a team, their company just shipped something, and they have a problem they want the new hire to solve. When you know those things, you can answer the question behind the question instead of the one on the surface.

It also settles the hardest part of preparing: which of your stories to tell. You stop rehearsing everything and start with the two or three that meet their problem.

What should you look for?

You can do this by hand. It comes in three parts, and they follow the clock.

Before the call: read it the night before

  • Who they are. Their title, how long they have been in the role, and the path that got them there. Use their LinkedIn and the company's own pages, and note where each fact came from.
  • How they talk. Search their name with "podcast", "YouTube" or "keynote", and read a few of their LinkedIn posts. A few minutes of hearing them speak tells you their style: big picture or detail, formal or relaxed, and the words they use. Use those words in your answers.
  • Their company right now. Two or three things that changed in the last few months: a launch, a partnership, research they published. The newsroom or blog is the place to look.
  • Your angle. One sentence on where your experience meets their problem, and the stories that back it up.

In the room: know the themes

  • The themes they will probe. Two or three topics their job and the posting point to.
  • For each theme, the questions and your answer. Start from the situation they care about, then give your own example, with its result.
  • Your questions for them, grouped by what they show: about the product, about their team, about the role.
  • One thing to mention that shows you did the homework.

After the call: within 24 hours

  • A short thank-you note that picks up something they said.
  • Write down the two hardest questions while they are fresh.
  • If you hear nothing, follow up after five working days.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Reading it like a script. The brief tells you what may come up. Your answers still need to sound like you.
  • Reciting their profile back to them. Use one fact to connect, not five to impress.
  • Going personal. Stick to their work: role, background and what they have shared publicly about their job.
  • Using a fact you cannot trace. Every fact in the brief shows its source. If you cannot see where something came from, check it before you use it.
  • Skipping the note. The thank-you is ready to edit. Send it the same day.

What if they are not on LinkedIn?

You can still write the brief from the three fields. The part about who they are will be thinner, so look for a bio on their company's site and read it yourself.

Will they know I looked at their LinkedIn?

They may, depending on your LinkedIn privacy settings. Looking up the person you are meeting is normal before an interview.

I am meeting three people. Do I need three briefs?

Yes, one per person. Each one owns something different, so each will probe different themes.
