How to write a LinkedIn headline that recruiters find

Your headline is the line next to your name in every LinkedIn search. Most people keep the default, their current title and company. Here is how to make it say the job you want next, in the words recruiters type.

Why does your LinkedIn headline matter?

Your headline is the line under your name. It shows in LinkedIn search results and in Google results for your name (Teal, 2022). When a recruiter searches for a role, the list shows your photo, your name and the start of your headline. That is often all they read before they decide whether to open your profile.

Recruiters also search by skills, not only by title. In LinkedIn's own survey of 1,271 recruiting leaders, companies whose recruiters searched by skills the most were 12% more likely to make a quality hire (LinkedIn, The Future of Recruiting 2025). The skills in your headline are words they can find you by.

What goes in a LinkedIn headline?

You can write it by hand. A headline that recruiters find has up to six parts, in this order, joined with a vertical bar.

  • A. The job you want. The title of your next job, the way most postings for it write it. One title only. Leave out "Aspiring" and "Open to work": LinkedIn has its own Open to Work setting for that.
  • B. Two to four skills. The words a recruiter for that kind of job would type. Take the ones that come up across the postings you are targeting, not from your own job description.
  • C. One or two industries. Where your experience sits, such as Consumer Goods or SaaS. A business model counts too: B2B, DTC, Marketplaces.
  • D. One result with a number. Short, like "Grew online sales 3x". The full story goes in your About section.
  • E. Names that back you. Optional. Past employers a recruiter would recognize, written as "ex-Company". Never use "ex-" for the company you work at now.
  • F. Something human. Optional. A short, safe-for-work line people remember, like "Marathon runner".

How to find the right words

Your headline is one line for every job you apply to. Unlike your resume, you don't change it for each application, so it should fit what your target jobs have in common.

  • Line up the jobs you plan to apply for. Five to ten postings is enough. Note the title most of them use and the skills that show up in most of them. That overlap is your headline.
  • Search the title on LinkedIn and read the headlines of people who already have that job.
  • Check every short form. Keep an abbreviation only if most of those postings use it. Recruiters type the words they see in postings.
  • Count your characters. LinkedIn allows 220. Put the job and your top skills first, since a search list cuts off the rest.

When it is ready, paste it in LinkedIn: open your profile, press the pencil by your name, then edit Headline. Put the same title at the top of your resume too, so both say the same thing. Your resume is the one you tailor to each job.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Keeping the default. Your current title and company say where you are, not where you want to go.
  • Changing it for every application. Your headline is one for all of them. Tailor your resume to each job instead.
  • Two job titles. A recruiter can't tell which one you want. Keep one, at the start.
  • Filler words. "Passionate", "results-driven" and "guru" take room, and nobody searches for them.
  • Short forms nobody types. Your company's internal names mean nothing to a recruiter. Use the words in postings.
  • A result you can't back up. If it is in your headline, expect to be asked about it in an interview.
  • "ex-" for your current company. It tells people you have already left.

How long can a LinkedIn headline be?

220 characters. A search list shows only the start, so put the job you want and your top skills first.

Should my headline say my current job or the one I want?

The one you want next, as long as your experience backs it up. Your current job is already on your profile, under Experience.

Should I change my headline for each job I apply to?

No. Your headline is one for every job you apply to, so write it for what those jobs have in common. Tailor your resume to each application instead.

Should I write "Open to work" in my headline?

No. It takes room and nobody searches for it. Use LinkedIn's own Open to Work setting instead.
